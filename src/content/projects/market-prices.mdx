## Context and constraints

Colombia's national statistics office publishes wholesale agricultural prices every week
through SIPSA. The data is public, authoritative, and free. It is also published as bulletins
built for a person to read: spreadsheets whose sheet names, column positions and header rows
move between releases, whose units are declared per sheet rather than per dataset, and whose
history exists only as a pile of separate files. There is no API and no contract. The source
will not change to suit you, and it will not tell you when it changes.

The business problem underneath was simpler than the data problem. Buyers negotiated with
suppliers with no external reference for what the market was doing — only internal history,
which tells you what you paid, not what you should have paid. What was missing was a benchmark
and a forward view.

## Architecture decisions

Ingestion is written on the assumption that the source will drift, because it does. The parser
resolves structure by content rather than by position, and unit normalisation happens **per
sheet, not once globally** — a decision that looks like over-engineering until you meet the
bulletin that declares a different unit on one sheet than on all the others. Everything lands
in Bronze exactly as received, so when a parsing assumption turns out to be wrong the fix is a
reprocess rather than a re-scrape of data that may no longer be available.

The lake is Delta Lake rather than flat Parquet, and the reason is narrow and specific:
**incremental upserts**. A weekly bulletin frequently restates prior weeks. With plain Parquet
that means rewriting partitions and hand-rolling the merge logic; with Delta it is a merge
operation with transactional semantics. This is the kind of decision I want to be able to
defend in one sentence — the feature that justified the dependency, not the general reputation
of the technology.

Polars is the processing engine throughout. On a single machine with these volumes, its
lazy-execution model does the work in a fraction of the memory that a pandas-shaped pipeline
would need, which matters when the machine is shared.

Forecasting is autoregressive to 52 weeks per product, with model selection by **multi-horizon
MAPE** rather than a single-step score. A model that is excellent at week one and useless at
week thirty is worse than one that is merely good throughout, because a buyer planning a
season is reading the far end of the curve. Scoring across horizons is what makes the selection
match the use.

The interface is FastAPI with HTMX. There is no single-page application, because there is no
requirement that needs one and there is one engineer to maintain it. HTMX gives partial updates
over the wire with server-rendered HTML, which means no separate frontend build, no state
duplicated across a client store, and no framework upgrade treadmill.

## The hard part

Two things, one technical and one about what the product turned out to be.

**The generative layer had to be made economical before it could be useful.** A language model
writing the reading of each series — what moved, how it compares to the internal price, what
the forecast implies — is the feature that turns a chart into something a buyer acts on. It is
also a per-view cost, and the same series gets viewed repeatedly without changing. So insights
are cached in Delta Lake keyed by a **hash of the payload the model was given**. Identical input
means the answer already exists; the call is never paid for twice. Keying on the payload rather
than on the product means the cache invalidates itself correctly the moment the underlying data
moves, with no expiry heuristic to tune. I wrote the pattern up as reusable, and it has since
been the default shape for LLM features elsewhere in my work.

**The unit bug is the one I tell in interviews.** Milled rice prices were being ingested an
order of magnitude off, and had been for a long stretch of history, because that product's
sheet in the DANE bulletin declares its unit differently from every other sheet. Nothing
crashed. The series looked like a series. It was just wrong, and it was wrong in a way that
would have quietly poisoned any negotiation that leaned on it. Finding it meant diffing against
an external reference rather than trusting internal consistency — a series can be perfectly
self-consistent and perfectly wrong. The fix was the per-sheet normalisation described above
plus a full historical backfill of the affected series.

The product shift was the other surprise. This was built as an internal analysis tool. This
year it was put on the internet behind authentication, because the commercial team wanted it in
the room during supplier negotiations. That changed what the project is: an internal notebook
does not need auth, uptime, or a story about who can see what, and a tool people negotiate with
does.

## Trade-offs

**No orchestrator, on purpose — for now.** The weekly run is a manual sequence rather than a
Dagster job, which is inconsistent with how my other pipelines work. Adding orchestration is
maybe a day of work and it is deliberately not done yet: the ingest is still the part most
likely to need a human when the source drifts, and automating around an unstable boundary buys
you unattended failure rather than unattended success. It gets orchestrated when the parser
stops surprising me.

**Cached insights can be stale by design.** If the model would now write a better paragraph
about unchanged data, the cache will not notice, because the cache key is the data and not the
model version. That is the right default at this cost profile, and the escape hatch is a
version component in the key when a model change actually warrants re-spending.

**HTMX over a SPA.** Less client-side interactivity than a React application would give, in
exchange for one deployable, one language in the hot path, and no build step on the frontend.
For a data tool with a single maintainer this has been the right side of the trade every time
it has come up.

## What the numbers are

Hundreds of products are covered with several years of price history each, forecast 52 weeks
ahead, with hundreds of automated tests running in CI. The platform moved from an internal tool
to an authenticated, internet-facing application used in supplier negotiation this year.
