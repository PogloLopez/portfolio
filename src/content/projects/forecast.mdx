## Context and constraints

Mercaldas is a retail grocery chain: more than a dozen stores, tens of thousands of active
items, and a replenishment decision to make every week for each combination of the two. That
decision was driven by the forecasting module bundled into the ERP — a black box in the strict
sense. It produced a number, it did not produce a reason, and there was no parameter anyone
inside the company could turn to make it better. When a store ran out of something, the honest
answer to "why did the forecast miss?" was that nobody could know.

The constraints on the replacement were unglamorous and they shaped everything. There is no
cloud budget and no GPU: the pipeline runs on a shared Windows server that also does other
work. There is no data team; there is me. That second constraint matters more than the first,
because it rules out any design whose failure mode is "someone notices and fixes it." Whatever
I built had to fail loudly on its own, because there is no second pair of eyes.

## Architecture decisions

The lake is a medallion layout — Bronze, Silver, Gold — on the local filesystem rather than on
object storage. Delta Lake gives the layer semantics without a warehouse behind it. Bronze is
raw and append-only, so a bad transform is always recoverable by replaying from it; Silver
holds cleaned series with the calendar and promotional features attached; Gold holds the
training frames the models actually consume. The reason to keep these separate on a single
server, where they could all be one script, is that the boundaries are where you put assertions.

The modelling layer is a **tournament rather than a model**. Series in a grocery catalogue do
not behave alike: a fast-moving cleaning product and a slow, lumpy durable good need different
machinery. Series are segmented by K-Means and the main workhorse is LightGBM per cluster, but
intermittent demand goes to Croston and SBA through `statsforecast`, because tree models on a
mostly-zero series learn to predict zero and are technically right and operationally useless.

The part I care about more than the model choice is the **switching rule**. A champion/challenger
setup where the criterion for promoting a challenger is "it looked better" degenerates into the
engineer's taste within two quarters. So the criterion is defined in the pipeline, measured on
held-out horizons, and applied by the job. MLflow keeps the runs so a promotion can be argued
about afterwards with the evidence still attached.

Orchestration is Dagster on a weekly schedule, with daily ingestion as a separate asset. That
split is the origin of the most interesting thing that has happened to this system.

## The hard part

Some months in, I decided to audit the pipeline. Not because anything was on fire — everything
was reporting success — but because "everything reports success" is exactly the state in which
a silent failure is invisible, and I had no independent measurement of whether the forecast was
still any good.

What the audit measured was the *usable horizon*: how many weeks ahead the system could still
forecast from the data it actually had. That number had been shrinking for weeks.

The cause turned out to be structural rather than a bug in the ordinary sense. The daily
ingestion job had stopped running. The weekly forecast job declared a dependency on the ingested
data, but the dependency was declared and not enforced: Dagster was happy to materialise the
forecast from whatever was already in the lake. So the weekly job ran, succeeded, and produced a
forecast — from data that was progressively more stale, losing a week of real horizon for every
week that passed. Every green checkmark in the system was accurate. The system was still wrong.

Two things came out of it. The immediate fix was to make the dependency real, so that a stale
upstream stops the downstream job instead of quietly degrading it. The durable fix was the audit
itself: horizon health is now measured every week as an independent signal, not inferred from
job status. The distinction is the whole lesson — **a pipeline's own success reporting cannot be
the evidence that a pipeline is working**, because the failure modes that survive longest are
precisely the ones the reporting does not cover.

I would rather show this than a leaderboard score. A model that beats a baseline on a held-out
set is table stakes. Finding your own silent production failure before the business does, by
building the instrument that could see it, is the part of the job that is actually hard.

## Trade-offs

**Pipeline reliability before model sophistication.** Hierarchical reconciliation — forcing
store-level forecasts to sum consistently to category and chain level — is not implemented. It
is a real gap and a well-understood technique. It is also the wrong next investment while the
base pipeline's failure modes are not fully instrumented. A reconciled forecast built on data
you cannot vouch for is a more confident version of the same problem.

**Local filesystem over cloud storage.** This buys zero infrastructure cost and no vendor
dependency, and it costs horizontal scale and a straightforward disaster-recovery story. At the
current data volume that trade is clearly right; it is also the decision most likely to be
revisited first, and it is documented as such rather than left as a discovery for whoever
inherits the system.

**Weekly cadence over daily.** Replenishment operates weekly, so a daily forecast would be
precision the business cannot consume. Matching the model's cadence to the decision's cadence
also keeps the compute inside the window the shared server can actually give it.

## What the numbers are

Against the ERP baseline it replaced, the system cut WMAPE by a double-digit percentage across
several product categories, with the largest gains in fast-moving lines such as household
cleaning and durables. Thousands of product×store series are forecast every week across more
than a dozen stores, and the output feeds the merchandise reordering system directly rather
than landing in a report someone has to read. The test suite is over 350 cases and is green
before anything deploys.

Business figures are given as ranges deliberately: the company is named, the exact numbers
are not mine to publish.
